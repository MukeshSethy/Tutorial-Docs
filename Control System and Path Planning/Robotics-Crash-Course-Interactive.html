PART I · THE LANGUAGE OF FEEDBACK
What a control system is, how we describe it, and what decides whether a robot settles down or shakes.

1Open Loop vs Closed Loop

In plain words. Open loop is throwing a dart with your eyes shut: you aim, you throw, and you never find out where it landed. Closed loop is throwing with your eyes open — you see where it went and adjust.

Open loop means you send a command and trust it worked. There is no check. If you tell Scout's left motor "60% power" because that gave 1 metre per second yesterday, you are running open loop. It works until something changes — and on a real robot, something always changes.

Closed loop adds a sensor and a subtraction. You measure what actually happened, subtract it from what you wanted, and get a number called the error. The controller then works on shrinking that error. Everything else in this document — every controller, every stability rule — is about how you turn that error into a command.

Scout's wheel speed loop. This exact picture is the running example for Parts I to III.
On Scout Scout is asked for 1.0 m/s. The encoder reports 0.8 m/s because it has just started up a wheelchair ramp. The error is 0.2 m/s, so the controller pushes harder. Notice that the controller never learns there is a ramp — it only sees that the number is short, and pushes. That is the whole beauty and the whole limitation of feedback.

There is one subtlety worth noticing in the diagram: the loop closes around the sensor, not around reality. If Scout's encoder is miscounting by 10%, the controller will drive the wheel to a confidently wrong speed and report complete success. You control the measurement, not the world.

Why it matters: feedback does not need your model of the robot to be correct. It only needs to know which direction the error is in. That is why it copes with a sagging battery, a warm bearing and an unexpected parcel all at once — they all arrive as the same thing, an error — and it is why you pay for it with a sensor and the risk of instability.

Q: Suppose Scout's wheels were driven by stepper motors instead. They hit the commanded position perfectly in the workshop, then lose accuracy in the building under load. What is going on?

A. It is open loop: the steps are assumed to have happened, never measured, so missed steps are permanent and invisible ✓ Names the structural cause. The fix is an encoder or more torque headroom — not tuning.

B. The controller gains need retuning ✗ There is no controller to tune — that is the point. The word "controller" is doing unearned work.

C. The integral term has wound up ✗ Windup is real and popular, which is why it is offered — but it needs an integrator, which an open-loop stepper does not have.

D. Sensor noise is corrupting the feedback ✗ There is no feedback to corrupt. Many wrong answers in control interviews assume a loop that is not there.

2Three Ways to Describe How Scout Moves

In plain words. Engineers write the same physics three different ways, because each one makes a different job easy.

Take Scout's left wheel. Push it with a torque and it speeds up — but not instantly, because the wheel and the robot have inertia. And it does not speed up forever, because friction grows with speed until the two balance.

1. The ODE (Ordinary Differential Equation). This is the raw physics: "inertia times how fast the speed is changing, plus friction times the current speed, equals the torque you applied". It is exact, and it is awkward to work with once you have several parts connected together.

2. The transfer function. A mathematical trick (the Laplace transform) turns "rate of change" into "multiply by s". Suddenly calculus becomes algebra. Two components in a row just multiply, and a whole feedback loop collapses into one fraction. This is the form in which people talk about speed and stability.

3. State space. Instead of one input and one output, you keep a list of everything going on inside — for Scout, that might be left wheel speed, right wheel speed, heading, and heading rate — and write how each one changes. This is the form you need once things are coupled together.

ODE                J × (rate of change of speed)  +  b × speed  =  torque

Transfer function  G(s) = speed out / torque in = 1 / (J s + b)

State space        ẋ = A x + B u      x is the LIST of internal quantities
                   y  = C x + D u      y is what the sensor can see
On Scout Driving in a straight line, each wheel is its own simple one-in-one-out problem, and a transfer function is plenty. But the moment Scout turns, the two wheels are no longer independent — speeding up the left wheel changes the heading, which changes what the right wheel needs to do. That coupling is exactly what a single-input, single-output transfer function cannot express, and it is why the arm on top, with five joints tugging on each other, needs state space.

There is a second, sharper reason to prefer state space: a transfer function only shows you what comes out. It is possible for something inside to be growing out of control while the input-output view looks perfectly calm, because the two effects cancel on the way out. State space keeps every internal quantity visible, so nothing can hide.

Why it matters: a transfer function tells you what comes out; a state-space model tells you what is going on inside. Scout's wheels alone are fine with the first. Scout's arm, where joints pull on each other, needs the second.

Q: How can a transfer function hide a problem that a state-space model shows?

A. Transfer functions assume zero initial conditions ✗ True, but irrelevant — that affects the wobble at the start, not whether something grows forever.

B. Transfer functions only work below the sampling frequency ✗ Mixes up continuous modelling with digital sampling — a different topic, borrowed to sound technical.

C. A pole and a zero can cancel, removing a runaway part from the input-output view while it still grows internally ✓ The real mechanism. That part is either unreachable by your motors or invisible to your sensors — hidden from outside, still growing inside.

D. Transfer functions cannot represent integrators ✗ They represent them perfectly well: 1/s. Confident and simply wrong.

3Poles and Zeros — Where Behaviour Lives

In plain words. Hit a bell and it rings at its own note, then fades. Every mechanical thing has notes like that. A pole is one of those natural notes: it says how fast something fades and how much it wobbles on the way. The s-plane is just a graph where you dot those notes down.

Why a graph? Because a pole is really two numbers at once. One says how quickly it dies away, and the other says how fast it wobbles while dying. So you plot them on two axes. The horizontal axis is the fading, the vertical axis is the wobbling.

The rule is then remarkably simple. If a pole sits on the left side, whatever it describes shrinks away — good. If it sits on the right, it grows without limit — the robot is out of control. That is the entire stability test: are all the notes on the left?

Two more useful facts fall out of the picture. First, a pole sitting far to the left fades so quickly that you never see it — so a complicated machine ends up behaving like a simple one, governed by whichever pole is nearest the middle. That one is called the dominant pole. Second, poles that appear in pairs above and below the line are the ones that wobble; a single pole on the line just fades smoothly without any bouncing.

On Scout Scout's wheel has several poles — the motor's electrical response (very fast, far left), the wheel and gearbox (medium), and the whole robot's mass (slowest, nearest the middle). When you watch Scout accelerate, you are essentially only seeing that last one. This is why the two numbers in §4 are usually enough to describe the whole robot.

A zero is the opposite of a pole: a frequency the system refuses to pass through. Zeros mostly just shape the response, with one important exception. A zero on the right side makes the output move the wrong way first — and no amount of clever control can fix it. The classic example is steering a bicycle: to turn right you must briefly steer left. Scout does not have this problem, but an aircraft does — pull back to climb and it briefly sinks, because the elevator must push the tail down before the wing can make the extra lift — and so does the flexible tip of a long arm.

Why it matters: the s-plane converts "how will this robot behave?" into "where are the dots?". Left means it calms down, up and down means it wobbles, right means it fails. Almost every design decision later in this document is really about dragging dots leftward.

4How Fast, and How Much Wobble

In plain words. Almost every loop you tune is shaped to behave like a car suspension: a spring that wants to get there, and a shock absorber that stops it bouncing. Two numbers describe it — how eager the spring is, and how much shock absorber you fitted.

The eagerness is called the natural frequency, written ωn. Bigger means faster. The amount of shock absorber is the damping ratio, written ζ (zeta). At zero it bounces forever. At one it glides in with no bounce at all. Between the two, it overshoots and then settles.

Almost everything you care about follows from those two numbers:

On Scout The wheel speed loop is tuned near ζ = 0.7 — a small overshoot nobody notices, and the quickest settling. The arm joints are tuned closer to ζ = 1, because an arm that overshoots by 4% while reaching into a shelf hits the shelf. Same theory, different requirement, because the consequence of overshoot is different.

Why it matters: ζ = 0.707 is the default because it settles soonest on the common 5% criterion (on a stricter 2% band the quickest is nearer ζ ≈ 0.78 — worth knowing that “fastest settling” depends on the band you measure to) for only a few percent overshoot — and it corresponds to about 65 degrees of "phase margin", the safety number in §16. Being able to say those are the same thing seen two ways is exactly what interviewers check.

Q: Scout's arm settles too slowly, but overshoot is completely forbidden. What helps?

A. Reduce ζ toward 0.5 ✗ It makes the rise faster but not the settling — at a fixed ωn, 2% settling is about 8/ωn at ζ = 0.5 versus about 5.8/ωn at ζ = 1 — and it spends the exact thing you were told you could not spend.

B. Sample more slowly to reduce noise ✗ Sounds like a sensible trade-off, but slower sampling adds delay, which is worse on both counts.

C. Add integral action ✗ Integral removes leftover error, not slowness — and it usually makes overshoot worse.

D. Increase ωn while keeping ζ at 1 or above ✓ At a fixed ζ, settling time scales as 1/ωn, so a bigger ωn shrinks it, while ζ ≥ 1 keeps overshoot at exactly zero. (Do not reach for 4/(ζωn) here: that is the under-damped approximation, and taken literally it claims more damping always settles sooner — false beyond ζ ≈ 0.78.) The two knobs are independent — that is the whole question.

Working out ζ — three ways, depending on what you already know

ζ is not a setting you type in anywhere. It is a consequence — of the hardware you have and the gains you chose. Every second-order system, whatever it is physically made of, can be rewritten into one standard shape, and once it is in that shape ζ is simply read off:

x'' + 2*z*wn*x' + wn^2 * x  =  wn^2 * target
       \\_______/    \\____/
        damping     stiffness

x'' = acceleration,  x' = speed,  wn = natural frequency,  z = zeta

1. From the physical parts, if you happen to know them — a mass m on a spring k with a damper c:

wn = sqrt(k / m)
z  = c / (2 * sqrt(k * m))

2. From your own gains — this is the one worth memorising, because it tells you what tuning actually does. For a joint with inertia J driven by torque = Kp·e + Kd·(de/dt):

wn = sqrt(Kp / J)
z  = Kd / (2 * sqrt(Kp * J))
Read that second line again: Kp is in the denominator of ζ. Double your P gain and ωn rises by √2 — faster — but ζ falls by √2 — wobblier. That is not bad luck or bad tuning; it is arithmetic. It is exactly why turning P up alone always eventually rings, and why D exists: Kd is the only term in that expression that can put ζ back up.

3. Backwards, from what you measured — the honest case, because on real hardware you rarely know J. Command a step, measure how far past the target it went as a fraction of the step itself, and solve:

OS = exp( -pi * z / sqrt(1 - z^2) )        # overshoot, as a fraction

z  = -ln(OS) / sqrt( pi^2 + ln(OS)^2 )     # the same thing, rearranged
import math

def zeta_from_overshoot(os_fraction):
    """0.37 means it went 37% of the step past the target."""
    L = math.log(os_fraction)
    return -L / math.sqrt(math.pi**2 + L**2)

def zeta_from_gains(Kp, Kd, J):
    """J is the inertia this loop is actually pushing."""
    return Kd / (2.0 * math.sqrt(Kp * J))

def settling_time(zeta, wn):
    """Roughly, to within 2% of target."""
    return 4.0 / (zeta * wn)

The practical loop: command a step, measure the overshoot, convert it to ζ with the formula above, and you now know where you are without knowing a single thing about your robot's inertia. Want less overshoot? Raise Kd. Want it faster? Raise Kp — and expect to raise Kd again afterwards to pay for it.

5Leftover Error, and Why Memory Fixes It

In plain words. Some controllers get you nearly there and stop. Adding a memory — something that keeps adding up how wrong you have been — is what closes the last gap.

Here is the problem, on Scout. Suppose the controller only multiplies the error by a gain. When Scout reaches exactly 1.0 m/s the error is zero, so the command is zero too. But Scout is on a ramp and needs real power just to hold that speed. So it slows down until the leftover error, multiplied by the gain, happens to produce exactly enough power. There is always a gap, and a bigger gain only makes it smaller, never zero.

An integrator solves this by remembering. Every cycle it adds the current error onto a running total, and that total drives the motor too. As long as any error remains, the total keeps growing and the push keeps increasing — so it cannot stop until the error is genuinely zero.

Memories in the loopHolding a fixed speedFollowing a changing speedOn Scout
NoneAlways a gapHopelessScout sags on every ramp
OneExactLags behind by a fixed amountScout holds 1.0 m/s on the ramp, but lags while accelerating
TwoExactExactRisky — each memory makes the loop more likely to oscillate

Why it matters: an integrator removes leftover error by definition, not by good tuning. If the loop has settled, the memory has stopped changing, and the only thing that stops it changing is zero error. The cost is that memories always react late, which is what makes loops unstable — the trade-off that runs through all of Part II and Part III.

PART II · PID CONTROL, IN DEPTH
The controller running Scout's wheels, its arm, and almost every robot ever shipped.

6PID — Three Views of One Error

In plain words. PID stands for Proportional, Integral, Derivative — three ways of looking at the same mistake. P asks "how wrong am I right now?" I asks "how long have I been wrong?" D asks "am I getting closer or further away?" Add the three answers together and that is your motor command.

Let us build it up on Scout's left wheel, which is asked for 1.0 m/s.

P — the muscle. The wheel is at 0.8 m/s, so the error is 0.2. Multiply by a gain and push. Twice as wrong means twice as hard. This alone gets you most of the way there, quickly — but as §5 showed, it always stops short, and turning the gain up to compensate makes Scout lurch and overshoot.

I — the memory. Every cycle, add the current error onto a running total. If Scout has been 0.05 m/s slow for two seconds, that total has built up into a real push, which is exactly what is needed to climb the ramp. This is what closes the final gap. The cost is that it reacts to history, so it is always a little behind — and it can build up far too much, which is §7.

D — the brakes. Look at how fast the error is shrinking. If Scout is rushing toward 1.0 m/s very quickly, D pushes back before arrival, so it does not sail past. This is the only one of the three that makes the loop safer rather than riskier. The cost is that it is extremely sensitive to noise, which is §8.

The two lines worth memorising. error_sum += error * dt is the integral — that is why it has memory. (error - last_error) is the derivative — that is why it sees a trend, and also why a single noisy encoder reading throws it wildly off. Every problem in the next two sections comes from one of these two lines.
On Scout In practice Scout's wheel loop runs PI only — P and I, no D. Wheel speed from an encoder is noisy enough that the derivative term causes more trouble than the damping is worth, and the loop is well damped anyway. The arm joints, which must not overshoot into shelves, do use D. Knowing that "most robots run PI, not PID" is a small but real credibility marker.

Why it matters: P gets you there fast but stops short. I closes the gap but reacts late, which risks instability. D adds damping and safety margin but amplifies noise. Nearly every tuning decision in robotics is choosing where to sit in that triangle.

Q: Scout reaches speed quickly but always settles 5% slow whenever it carries a heavy parcel. First fix?

A. Increase the P gain ✗ It shrinks the gap — but never to zero, and enough P gain to make it small makes Scout lurch instead.

B. Add or increase the I gain ✓ A gap that appears under load is the signature of a missing memory. Only accumulated error can keep pushing once the present error is tiny.

C. Add D gain ✗ D only reacts to change, and a steady 5% gap is not changing — D sees nothing at all.

D. Run the loop faster ✗ That computes the same wrong answer more often. Speed affects stability, not leftover error.

7Integral Windup — When the Memory Overflows

In plain words. Imagine pushing a car stuck against a kerb. You push harder and harder, getting more frustrated — that frustration is the integral building up. When the car finally comes free it shoots off, because you are still pushing with all that stored effort. That is windup.

Here is how it happens to Scout. It is asked to climb a steep ramp, and it simply does not have enough motor power. The error stays at, say, 0.3 m/s for four seconds. The motor is already at 100% — asking for more achieves nothing — but error_sum += error * dt does not know that, so the memory keeps growing, second after second.

Then Scout crests the ramp onto flat floor. Suddenly it has plenty of power, and the controller is sitting on an enormous stored command. Scout accelerates far past 1.0 m/s. And the only way that memory shrinks is by accumulating error in the opposite direction — which means Scout must stay too fast for a while before it comes back down.

The fix, in every case, is to stop the memory growing when growing it cannot possibly help. The neatest version is three lines of logic:

float raw     = Kp*error + Ki*error_sum + Kd*slope;
float command = clamp(raw, 0.0f, 1.0f);            /* what the motor can do   */

int saturated  = (command != raw);                 /* we asked for the impossible */
int pushing_in = ((error > 0) == (raw > command)); /* and error makes it worse */

if (!(saturated && pushing_in))
    error_sum += error * dt;                       /* only remember useful error */
MethodHow it works, and when to use it
ClampingPut a hard ceiling on error_sum. The one-line fix. Size the ceiling so the integral alone can just hold Scout on the steepest ramp it will ever meet
Conditional integrationThe code above — freeze the memory while the motor is maxed out. The usual choice in motion control
Back-calculationFeed the difference between what you asked for and what you got back into the memory. Smoothest recovery, one more number to tune
Reset on a big new commandWipe the memory when the target jumps a long way. Crude but effective when moves are large and rare

Why it matters: windup is the single most common PID bug in the field, and it always looks like "the robot overshoots sometimes but not always". The giveaway is that it only happens after the motor has been saturated — after a ramp, a heavy load, or a big commanded jump. Say "I size the integral limit so it alone can just hold the worst-case load" and it reads as real experience.

8Why D Always Needs a Filter

In plain words. The derivative term has two separate problems, both fixable in one line each. It jumps violently when you change the target, and it massively magnifies sensor noise.

Problem 1 — the kick. Suppose Scout's target jumps from 0 to 1.0 m/s the instant a delivery starts. The error jumps by 1.0 in a single cycle, so (error - last_error)/dt is enormous — and for one cycle the motor gets slammed. The fix is neat: measure how fast the wheel is changing, not how fast the error is changing. For disturbances the two are identical, but when the target jumps, only the error jumps. The kick vanishes.

Problem 2 — noise. Scout's encoder has finite resolution, so at low speed the measured value flickers between neighbouring counts. To a derivative, a flicker of one count in one millisecond looks like an enormous acceleration. The D term dutifully sends that straight to the motor, which is why the symptom is an audible buzz and a hot motor. The fix is a gentle smoothing filter on the D path only.

/* both fixes, four lines */
float speed_now = (position - last_position) / dt;   /* POSITION loop (arm joint): the
   measurement is position, so its slope is speed. For a SPEED loop, difference the
   measured SPEED instead: D must be -Kd * d(measurement)/dt, not -Kd * speed  */
smoothed += (speed_now - smoothed) * (dt / (dt + FILTER_TIME));  /* smooth it   */
d_term    = -Kd * smoothed;          /* measure the WHEEL, not the error       */
last_position = position;            /* (hence the minus sign)                  */
On Scout This is exactly why Scout's wheel loop is PI rather than PID. At low speed the encoder gives only a handful of counts per cycle, so the derivative is mostly noise. Scout's arm joints have much finer encoders and must not overshoot, so they do use D — with a filter, and taken on the measurement.

Why it matters: you cannot simply filter harder to fix the noise, because filtering adds delay — and D exists precisely to act early. Over-filter and you pay the delay cost while getting none of the benefit. The usual compromise puts the filter about ten times faster than the D action itself.

Q: Adding D gain to Scout's arm makes the joint motor buzz audibly and run hot, even though the plotted response looks better. What is happening?

A. D is amplifying encoder noise straight into the motor — filter it, and take it on the measurement ✓ Names the physics: the buzz is noise reaching the motor. Fixes it without giving up the damping.

B. D is too high — halve it ✗ It would reduce the symptom, and that is the trap: it also throws away the damping you added D for. Treat the cause.

C. The PWM frequency is in the audible range ✗ A genuinely common cause of motor whine, which is why it is offered — but it would not have appeared the moment you changed D.

D. The loop runs too fast ✗ A slower loop would in fact make each finite-difference step less noisy, since the same one-count flicker is divided by a bigger dt — but it adds delay and throws away damping, and the real cause is unfiltered noise reaching the motor, not the loop rate.

Interview tip: The follow-up is "where else could you get speed?" — time the gaps between encoder pulses rather than counting pulses per cycle, which is far quieter when Scout is crawling.

9Running PID on a Real Computer

In plain words. Everything so far assumed time flows smoothly. Scout actually runs the controller in discrete steps — maybe every 2 milliseconds. That changes three things, and all three catch people.

1. The time step is secretly part of your tuning. Look back at the code: the I term multiplies by dt and the D term divides by it. If Scout's loop sometimes takes 2 ms and sometimes 3.5 ms because a navigation update stole time, then the effective I and D gains are changing cycle to cycle. You tuned one controller and are running a different one every step.

2. Sampling itself adds delay. Between steps the command is held frozen, which behaves like half a step of extra delay. And delay is what eats stability (§18). This is why "sample twice as fast as the signal" — the rule you may remember from signal processing — is nowhere near enough for control.

3. There are two ways to write the code. Both are correct; they fail differently.

/* POSITIONAL -- work out the whole command from scratch each cycle */
error_sum += error * dt;
command = Kp*error + Ki*error_sum + Kd*slope;    /* needs explicit anti-windup */

change   = Kp*(e - e1) + Ki*e*dt + Kd*(e - 2*e1 + e2)/dt;
command += change;
/* Here 'command' IS the memory, so clamping it gives anti-windup for free.
   The catch: it needs an I term. With Ki = 0 a constant error gives change = 0,
   so a P or PD controller in this form can sit at ANY output. Name that trade-off. */

10Tuning — What You Are Really Choosing

In plain words. Whatever ritual you follow, you are choosing just two things: how fast the loop responds, and how much safety margin it keeps. Every tuning method is a different way of arriving at those two.

The method almost everyone actually uses on a robot like Scout is by hand, in a specific order:

  1. P first. Set I and D to zero. Raise P until Scout responds briskly and just begins to wobble, then back off a little.
  2. D next. Add D until the wobble calms down. You can now usually raise P a bit further than before.
  3. I last, and least. Add the smallest I that removes the leftover gap on a ramp. Stop as soon as it does.

The reason for that order is that each term is being tuned against the misbehaviour the previous one introduced. Saying that sentence out loud is the tell that you have done it rather than read about it.

11Feedforward — Acting Before the Error Appears

In plain words. Feedback is steering by looking only in the mirror — you cannot correct until you have already drifted. Feedforward is looking through the windscreen: you can see the ramp coming, so you push harder before you slow down.

Feedback has a structural weakness: its input is the error, so an error must exist before it can act. On a fast move, that lag grows with speed. Feedforward sidesteps this entirely by computing what the move will need and adding it in up front.

For Scout, there are several things you can predict without any sensor at all:

  • Speed feedforward — you know Scout wants 1.0 m/s, and you roughly know what motor power gives 1.0 m/s. Apply that immediately rather than waiting for feedback to discover it.
  • Acceleration feedforward — the plan says accelerate at 0.5 m/s², and you know Scout's mass, so you know the extra push needed.
  • Friction feedforward — there is always a small push needed just to overcome stiction and get moving.
  • Gravity feedforward — on the arm, each joint needs a standing torque just to hold the parcel up against gravity, and that is computable from the joint angles.
Feedforward carries the load; feedback only handles the surprises — so feedback's job gets much smaller.
On Scout Gravity compensation on the arm is the highest-value feedforward of the lot. Without it, the joint holding a 2 kg parcel relies entirely on the integral term building up — slowly, and with all of §7's windup risk. With it, the standing torque is applied instantly, the integral stays near zero, and the loop keeps its safety margin.

Why it matters: feedforward sits outside the loop, so it cannot make you unstable — it is free, stability-wise. Two honest caveats, both of which this document raises elsewhere and an interviewer may well join up for you: a large feedforward term can drive the actuator into saturation, and a saturated actuator is an open loop with a winding-up integrator behind it (§7); and feedforward built by inverting the plant is only usable if that inverse is stable — invert a non-minimum-phase plant, one with a right-half-plane zero (§3), and the inverse blows up. Feedforward cannot destabilise the feedback loop itself; it can still ruin your day. The third and commonest risk is simply a wrong model sending a wrong push, which feedback then has to fight. That makes it the first thing to add and the cheapest win available.

12Cascade Control — Loops Inside Loops

In plain words. Russian dolls. The outer loop worries about position and tells the middle loop what speed to hold. The middle loop tells the inner loop how much current — and therefore how much force — to produce. Each loop is simple, and each runs faster than the one outside it.

Why not just write one clever controller that goes straight from "be at desk 14" to "motor volts"? Because that one controller would have to understand coil inductance, back-EMF, battery sag, gearbox friction, robot mass and floor grip all at once.

Cascade splits the problem so each layer solves one thing and hides it from the layer above. Once the current loop is closed and fast, the speed loop no longer sees any electrical behaviour at all — it just sees something that produces whatever force it asks for. Each layer therefore gets a simple plant, so each can be a boring, reliable PI.

Two rules govern the arrangement. First, each inner loop must be roughly 5 to 10 times faster than the one outside it. If they are similar speeds, the two start arguing with each other and you get behaviour neither tuning predicted. Second, you tune from the inside out — current, then speed, then position — because tuning an outer loop assumes the inner one already behaves perfectly.

On Scout The current, speed and joint-position loops run on the small microcontroller right next to the motors, at hundreds of hertz to kilohertz rates. The navigation layers run on the main computer at tens of hertz. That split is not an accident: the fast loops must never wait for an operating system that might pause to write a log file.
Bonus point they like: cascade gives safety limits for free. A current limit inside the innermost loop is a hard ceiling on force that no amount of position error can ever exceed — safety built into the structure, rather than a software check somebody might forget.

Why it matters: "current, then speed, then position" is the tell that you have actually commissioned a drive rather than read about one. And the 5× separation rule explains most cascade bugs: if the outer loop oscillates at the inner loop's own rate, they have got too close together.

Q: Scout's position loop oscillates at roughly the speed loop's natural rate. Most likely cause?

A. The position gain is simply too high ✗ Lowering it does stop the oscillation, which makes this feel right — but the diagnosis matters: the ceiling is set by the loop underneath, not by anything intrinsic.

B. Encoder resolution is too low ✗ Coarse encoders do cause small twitchy oscillation at low speed — but it would not land exactly on the inner loop's rate.

C. The two loops are now too close in speed and are interfering ✓ The oscillation happening at the inner loop's own rate is the giveaway. Fix by speeding up the inner loop or slowing the outer one, restoring the 5× gap.

D. The current loop has wound up ✗ Windup gives one big overshoot after saturation, not steady oscillation at a characteristic frequency.

"How would you tune a robot's drive from scratch?" — the 60-second spoken answer
I work from the inside out. First the current loop, which is really a torque loop, usually a PI at ten to twenty kilohertz — once that is closed and fast, everything outside it just sees something that produces whatever force it asks for. Then the speed loop, also PI, aiming for something between a fifth and a tenth of the current loop's rate so the two cannot interfere. Then position on top. Before touching a single gain I add feedforward: the rough power needed for the target speed, the extra for the planned acceleration, and on an arm, gravity worked out from the joint angles. Feedforward sits outside the loop so it cannot cost me stability, and it leaves feedback a much smaller job. For each loop I raise the proportional gain until it responds briskly and just starts to wobble, add derivative until that calms, then add the least integral that removes the leftover droop on a ramp — remembering integral always costs me safety margin. I cap the integral memory so it alone can just hold the worst-case load, and if I use derivative at all I take it on the measurement rather than the error, and filter it. Then I verify rather than assume: a step response for overshoot and settling, and a frequency sweep to confirm the margin is really there — at least forty-five degrees, and nearer sixty-five on any loop I want settled rather than merely stable.
PART III · STABILITY — WHY LOOPS SHAKE THEMSELVES APART
How close Scout is to trouble, how you measure that, and what quietly eats the margin.

13What "Unstable" Actually Means

In plain words. Think of pushing a child on a swing. Push at the right moment and it goes higher and higher. A feedback loop can do exactly this to itself — if the correction arrives at the wrong moment, it stops cancelling the error and starts adding to it.

Here is the mechanism, on Scout. The controller notices Scout is slow and pushes harder. But suppose everything in the loop — the encoder filter, the communication link, the motor's own sluggishness — means that extra push does not arrive at the wheel until half a wobble later. By then Scout is already going too fast, and your "speed up" command makes it worse. The controller then commands "slow down", which arrives when Scout is already too slow. Each round trip makes the swing bigger.

Instability needs two things to be true at once, which is good news because you only have to break one of them:

  • The signal must come back badly timed — delayed by half a wobble.
  • It must come back the same size or bigger. If it comes back smaller each time, the wobble dies out anyway.

Why it matters: this explains the single most counter-intuitive fact in control — turning the gain up can make a robot unstable even though nothing about the timing changed. A bigger gain means the signal is still large at higher frequencies, and higher frequencies are always more delayed. Gain does not create the bad timing; it walks you up into where the bad timing already lives.

14Root Locus — Watching the Poles Move

In plain words. A picture of what happens to Scout's natural notes (§3) as you slowly turn one gain up. It shows at a glance the gain where wobbling starts and the gain where it becomes unstable.

Why it matters: it teaches you the shape of every fix, which is what you actually reason with. Adding a zero to the left pulls the notes left and makes things safer — that is what D does. Adding a pole pushes them right and makes things riskier — that is what I does, and what every filter you add does too.

Look carefully at the root-locus picture above: with only two poles the branches go straight up and down and never reach the vertical line. That is a real result, not an artefact of the drawing — a two-pole loop with no delay cannot be made unstable by raising the gain, however far you push it. It just gets less and less damped. To actually cross you need a third pole, or a delay, or a right-half-plane zero — which is exactly why the delay section that follows matters so much, and why real loops go unstable while textbook second-order ones never do.

15The Bode Plot — Testing Every Speed at Once

In plain words. Open Scout's speed loop, wiggle the signal going into the controller slowly, and see how big and how late the measured wheel speed comes back round. Then wiggle faster. And faster. A Bode plot is just those results drawn on two graphs: how big the response is, and how far behind it lags.

Two things generally happen as you wiggle faster — generally, not always, because a lightly damped resonance makes the response briefly get bigger instead, which is the mast mode three sections further on. Away from a resonance: the response gets smaller, because the robot's mass cannot keep up. And it gets later, because everything in the chain takes time. Both curves head downward. That downward drift is what eventually kills you, and the Bode plot is where you watch it happen.

The single most important point on the plot is the crossover frequency — the wiggling speed at which the round-the-loop response is exactly life-size. Below it, the loop has authority and crushes errors. Above it, the loop has effectively given up and Scout is running open loop. So "how fast is your controller?" and "where is your crossover?" are the same question.

16Gain Margin and Phase Margin — Your Two Safety Numbers

In plain words. Both answer "how close is Scout to trouble?", but they measure different kinds of closeness. Gain margin: how much stronger could everything get before it goes unstable? Phase margin: how much more delay could it survive?

Recall from §13 that instability needs bad timing and enough size. The two margins simply measure your distance from each of those conditions:

  • Phase margin — go to the speed where the response is exactly life-size, and ask how much lag is still missing before it reaches half a wobble. That leftover is your margin, in degrees.
  • Gain margin — go to the speed where the lag has reached half a wobble, and ask how far below life-size the response is. That gap is your margin.

They are read at two different wiggling speeds, which is the part people get wrong.

17When the Robot Would Fall Over on Its Own

In plain words. Everything above assumes Scout would just sit there if you switched the controller off. Some robots would not — a self-balancing robot falls over. Those need a different test, called the Nyquist criterion.

Scout is a stable machine: turn everything off and it simply stops. But imagine a two-wheeled balancing version. Now the controller is not merely improving behaviour — it is supplying stability the machine does not have.

That leads to a genuinely startling consequence: too little gain is just as fatal as too much. There is a minimum gain below which the robot falls over. This is why you can never "turn the gains down to be safe" on a balancing robot, and why simply reading margins off a Bode plot gives the wrong answer for one.

Why it matters: almost everyone can recite "aim for 45 degrees of phase margin". Far fewer can add "but if the machine is unstable on its own, that test is not valid by itself — I would use Nyquist". That distinction is a reliable senior-level marker.

18Delay — the Silent Killer

In plain words. Delay is the most common cause of mysterious instability in real robots, and the hardest to spot — because it does not change the size of anything. It only makes everything late. And the faster you try to run, the worse it gets.

Why does running faster make delay worse? Because what matters is not the delay in milliseconds, but how much of a wobble it represents. A 5 millisecond delay is nothing if Scout's loop is wobbling once per second. But if the loop is wobbling 20 times per second, one wobble is only 50 milliseconds — so 5 milliseconds is a tenth of a wobble, and that is a significant chunk of your safety margin.

"Why did the loop go unstable?" — the 45-second spoken answer
A loop goes unstable when the correction comes back round delayed by about half a wobble and still the same size or bigger, because at that point it stops cancelling the error and starts adding to it. So I look at where the loop's response crosses life-size, and check how much timing margin is left there — that is phase margin, and I want forty-five to sixty degrees. There are three usual culprits. Too much gain, which does not create the bad timing but walks you up into where it already lives. And delay, which is the sneaky one, because it changes the size of nothing so it never shows on a magnitude plot — and the lag it costs grows the faster you run. The fixes come in that same order: reduce gain, reduce integral, hunt down and remove delay, or deliberately add lead right at crossover.
PART IV · BEYOND PID
State feedback, LQR, Kalman filters and MPC — what Scout needs once one knob is not enough.

20State Feedback — Watching Everything at Once

In plain words. A PID watches one number: the error. State feedback watches everything the robot is doing at once — position, speed, heading, turn rate — and blends them all into one command, each with its own gain.

Why would Scout need this? Because when it turns, the wheels are not independent. Speeding up the left wheel changes the heading, which changes what the right wheel should do, which changes the position. Two separate PIDs cannot see that — each one only knows its own wheel, so nothing is regulating heading at all unless you wrap another loop around them.

State feedback takes the list of everything — here, forward speed, heading, and turn rate — and produces each motor command from all of them:

MATHS                       IN CODE
u = -K x                    left  = k1*speed_err + k2*heading_err - k3*turn_rate;
                            right = k4*speed_err + k5*heading_err - k6*turn_rate;

x is the "state" -- the list of everything you track
K is a table of gains, one per state per motor

Those signs are the one subtlety, and they are worth getting right in an
interview.  u = -K x drives the STATE to zero.  But this document's error is
e = target - measured (section 6), so for anything that HAS a target,
e = -x: the two minus signs cancel and the error terms come out positive.
turn_rate has no target -- it is a raw state -- so it keeps its minus sign,
and that term is exactly what damps the rotation.

Because you now have one gain per tracked quantity, you have enough knobs to place every one of the system's natural notes (§3) exactly where you want — provided the motors can actually reach every one of them (§21). A PID gives you three knobs no matter how complicated the machine is; state feedback scales with the machine.

There is one catch that bites everyone. Look at the code: when every error is zero, the command is zero. So there is nothing left to hold Scout against a ramp — it sags exactly like a P-only controller. The fix is to add error_sum from §6 as one more tracked quantity, so the memory is part of the state.

Why it matters: the temptation is to place the notes very far left and get an infinitely responsive robot. Three bills arrive at once — the gains become huge so the motors saturate, huge gains blow sensor noise into the motors, and a very fast loop starts shaking mechanical parts you never modelled. "Place them where the hardware can actually go" is the mature answer, and it leads straight to LQR.

21Can You Reach It, Can You See It?

In plain words. Two sanity questions, and both are really about where you put your hardware. Controllable: can my motors actually influence everything that moves? Observable: can my sensors actually see everything that moves?

On Scout these are concrete questions. The LiDAR mast wobbles when Scout stops suddenly. Can the wheel motors do anything about that wobble? Only weakly — the same motors that excite it could in principle damp it, so strictly it is controllable, but their authority over it is so poor that in practice you treat it as uncontrollable, and the fix is mechanical or to stop exciting it (§27). Can any sensor see it? Only if the IMU is mounted on the mast rather than the chassis.

Why it matters: you do not actually need to control everything — only the things that would otherwise run away. A wobble that dies out on its own is fine even if you cannot touch it. Making that distinction ("I need it stabilisable, not fully controllable") is a senior-level answer, and it stops you adding hardware you do not need.

22LQR — Letting the Maths Choose the Gains

In plain words. LQR (Linear Quadratic Regulator) turns tuning into a question you can actually answer. Instead of "where should I put six gains?", it asks "how much do you care about accuracy, versus how much do you care about battery and motor wear?"

You fill in two tables of weights. One says how bad each kind of error is; the other says how expensive each motor's effort is. A solver then computes the gains that give the best possible balance.

cost = sum over time of  (how wrong am I)  +  (how hard am I working)
                          weighted by Q        weighted by R

For Scout:
   big weight on heading error  ->  it fights hard to stay pointing right
   big weight on motor effort   ->  it accepts drifting a little to save battery

A good starting point, called Bryson's rule, is to set each weight to one divided by the square of the largest value you would tolerate. If you can live with 2 degrees of heading error and 3 amps of current, those numbers set the weights — and everything becomes comparable, instead of you trying to weigh degrees against amps by feel.

Why it matters: plain LQR comes with a free guarantee of at least 60 degrees of phase margin, which is remarkable. The catch, which interviewers love, is that this guarantee vanishes completely the moment you add an observer to estimate things you cannot measure directly — which, on a real robot, you always do.

Q: An LQR-controlled Scout tracks nicely while driving but sags on ramps. Best fix?

A. Increase the accuracy weights ✗ It shrinks the sag and never removes it — the same story as raising the P gain, at the cost of more effort and more noise.

B. Add the running total of error as an extra tracked state, then redesign ✓ Plain state feedback produces nothing when the error is zero, so it cannot hold against a steady load. Adding the memory is the structural fix.

C. Reduce the effort weights to allow more push ✗ The same as option A by another route: it scales everything up without adding the memory the problem actually needs.

D. Run the loop faster ✗ A leftover gap is not a timing problem. This distractor appears everywhere because it always sounds productive.

23Observers and the Kalman Filter

In plain words. Scout needs to know which way it is facing. The encoders can tell you — but they drift when the wheels slip. The IMU can tell you — but it slowly drifts. An observer runs a little simulation of Scout alongside the real one and nudges it whenever the sensors disagree. A Kalman filter is the same thing, except it works out how hard to nudge automatically.

Here is the actual problem on Scout. Its wheel encoders say it has turned 90 degrees — but if a wheel slipped on a polished floor, that is wrong, and the error is permanent because it accumulates. Its gyroscope also measures turning, very smoothly, but it slowly drifts over minutes. Neither sensor is trustworthy alone. Together they are excellent, because their weaknesses are opposites: the gyro is right over short times and wrong over long ones; the encoders are right on average but jump when a wheel slips.

The simplest useful version is the complementary filter, which is genuinely one line of code:

MATHS                                   IN CODE (inside the loop)
angle = A·(angle + gyro_rate·dt)         heading = A * (heading + gyro_rate*dt)
      + (1-A)·encoder_angle                      + (1 - A) * encoder_heading;

A is typically 0.98 -- meaning "mostly trust the gyro moment to moment,
but let the encoders slowly pull the answer back to the truth"

A full Kalman filter does exactly the same job, but instead of a fixed 0.98 it recalculates the blend every single step, based on how uncertain it currently is versus how noisy each sensor is. If Scout is driving straight on carpet it trusts the encoders more; if a wheel just slipped it trusts them less — but only if you tell it. The gain is computed from the noise levels you give the filter, not from the measurements themselves, so a plain Kalman filter cannot notice a slip on its own: raise the encoder noise setting whenever Scout accelerates hard, or reject a reading that disagrees wildly with the prediction.

On Scout This is also how Scout gets a usable wheel speed without differentiating the encoder (§8). Rather than subtracting successive positions and getting noise, the observer's internal model supplies the smoothness, and the encoder only corrects it. Cleaner signal, no extra delay from filtering.

Why it matters: "a Kalman filter is an observer whose gain retunes itself every step, based on which source it currently trusts more" is the sentence that lands. And the standard rule is to make the observer 2 to 5 times faster than the controller — fast enough that the estimate settles before the controller acts on it, but not so fast that it just passes sensor noise straight through.

24MPC — Plan Ahead, Take One Step, Re-Plan

In plain words. MPC (Model Predictive Control) is how you drive a car. You look a few seconds ahead, work out a whole plan, then actually perform only the next fraction of a second of it — and then look again and re-plan. Throwing away most of the plan is the entire point, because the world will have moved.

What makes MPC different from everything else in this document is that limits are part of the question, not an afterthought. Every other controller computes a command and then clips it if it is too big, patching the damage with anti-windup. MPC is told the limits up front and will never plan to break them. Two caveats an interviewer will probe: motor limits are honoured exactly (the plan chooses the commands), but limits on where the robot ends up hold only as well as the model predicts, and if no plan can satisfy them all the solver has no answer at all — so those are usually made soft, a penalty rather than a wall.

On Scout Scout's wheel loops do not need MPC — PI is plenty. But when Scout must round a tight corner at the end of a corridor while carrying a tall stack of parcels, MPC is exactly right: it can see the corner coming and start slowing early, and it will respect "do not tip the load" as a hard limit rather than discovering it too late.

25Cancelling the Robot's Own Physics

In plain words. Scout's arm is much harder to move when stretched out than when folded, and swinging one joint tugs on the others. If you can calculate all that, you can add it in and cancel it out — and then every joint behaves like the same plain free mass — a puck on ice — whatever the pose.

The arm's physics has four parts: how heavy it feels right now (which depends on how folded it is), the tugging between joints, gravity, and friction. Computed torque means calculating all four, adding the coupling, gravity and friction terms to your command and multiplying the position-and-velocity correction by how heavy the arm currently feels (the inertia matrix), so what remains is a simple, identical problem at every joint. One set of gains then works everywhere in the workspace, instead of needing different gains folded versus extended.

In practice, the full version disappoints, because it is only as good as your model — and an unknown parcel in the gripper breaks the model immediately (it upsets the gravity term too, but that leftover is a slow, steady torque the PID's integral mops up, whereas a wrong inertia model corrupts every fast move). So the partial version wins: gravity compensation alone captures most of the benefit for a tiny fraction of the modelling work, which is why §11 called it the highest-value feedforward.

Why it matters: knowing when not to use the fancy method is the point. For Scout's arm carrying unknown parcels, full computed torque is a modelling project with poor returns; gravity compensation plus a well-tuned PID gets you 80% of the benefit next week.

"When would you use LQR or MPC instead of PID?" — the 60-second spoken answer
PID is my default, and for a single wheel speed loop it is the right answer — it needs no model and anyone on the team can maintain it. I move to state feedback and LQR when the states are genuinely coupled: on a differential-drive robot, once I care about heading as well as speed, the two wheels are not independent, and separate wheel PIDs simply cannot see that coupling. LQR also gives me a principled way to trade accuracy against effort, and those weights are questions I can answer physically — how many degrees of heading error is one amp worth — unlike choosing six gains by hand. I reach for MPC only when limits are the essence of the problem rather than an afterthought: rounding a corner with a tall load where "do not tip it" is a hard constraint, or anywhere I can see the future and want to brake early. I would say the costs out loud too. LQR needs a model, and its famous margin guarantee vanishes the moment I add an observer to estimate what I cannot measure. MPC needs a model plus an optimisation that must finish every single cycle, on a robot that also has to run a planner and a perception stack.

Four things that bite every control loop on a real machine. Short, because the algorithms matter more.

26What a Motor Actually Does

In plain words. Three facts explain the whole cascade from §12. Current makes force. A spinning motor fights back. And force, minus the load, is what accelerates Scout.
torque  =  Kt × current       <-- THE key fact: to control force, control current
back-EMF =  Ke × speed         <-- the faster it spins, the harder it pushes back

Torque is current. This is why a current loop is a force loop, and why it sits innermost and fastest in the cascade. Everything above it just asks for force and gets it.

Back-EMF (Electro-Motive Force) is why crude open-loop driving half-works: as Scout speeds up, the motor opposes the supply more, so current and torque naturally drop. It is accidental free speed regulation — and it stops holding the speed the instant you add a ramp or a heavy parcel: at fixed voltage the speed simply droops, falling in a straight line with load torque.

On Scout — brushed vs brushless Brushed motors switch their own coils mechanically. Brushless ones (BLDC) are more efficient but need software to do the switching, from the rotor position. FOC (Field-Oriented Control) does this by converting three messy phase currents into two tidy numbers — one that makes torque and one that does not — then running an ordinary PI on each. The point is the same as cascade control: after the transform, a complicated machine looks like a simple one.

Q: Scout’s elbow is holding a 2 kg parcel out at arm’s length. The joint holds the pose perfectly — the encoder barely moves — but after about thirty seconds the motor driver goes into thermal shutdown. Nothing is moving, so where is the heat coming from?

A. At near-zero speed the back-EMF is at its maximum, so the driver has to fight it with extra current. ✗ Backwards — back-EMF is proportional to speed, so at a standstill it is essentially zero. This is the most common back-EMF misconception.

B. The position loop’s P gain is too high; lower it so the motor stops demanding so much torque. ✗ The holding torque is set by the parcel and gravity, not by the gain. Lowering P just lets the joint sag while drawing much the same current.

C. Holding torque means holding current, and at zero speed there is no back-EMF and no mechanical output at all, so every watt the winding takes (I²R) becomes heat — while the driver conducts that same DC current continuously. ✓ Right — stall is a motor’s worst thermal case: the full holding current flowing continuously with zero mechanical output, parked in one winding or commutator bar, and no rotation to spread it around or cool it. Be precise about the mechanism if you are asked: the current loop chops the supply down so the winding only ever sees I × R, and what trips the driver is its own conduction loss at that current, not the winding’s. Size for continuous stall torque, or design the load away with more reduction, a brake, or a counterbalance.

D. Zero speed × torque is zero watts of mechanical work, so the heat must be gearbox friction rather than the motor. ✗ Zero mechanical output is precisely the problem: with no power leaving the shaft, all the electrical input has nowhere to go but heat in the windings.

27Never Command a Jump — Motion Profiles

In plain words. Telling Scout "be over there, now" asks for infinite acceleration. The motor maxes out, the integral winds up (§7), and the machine gets hit. Instead you feed it a smooth plan: speed up, cruise, slow down.

A sudden change in acceleration is a sudden change in force, and a sudden force rings every resonance in the machine at once. That is why Scout's LiDAR mast wobbles after a hard stop. Easing the acceleration in and out (an S-curve) strips most of the high-frequency content out of the force, so far less energy lands on those resonances.

Why it matters: always plan to below the motor's real limits — leave 20 to 30% headroom. If the plan already demands 100% of available torque, the feedback controller has nothing left to correct errors with, and the loop is effectively open for the whole move.

Q: Scout’s arm moves parcels with a trapezoidal velocity profile that respects the joint’s velocity and acceleration limits. The cruise phase is smooth, but right at each corner of the profile the gripper and its payload ring for roughly half a second, and the last centimetre of every move settles slowly. What is the right fix?

A. Raise the D gain on the joint so the ringing gets damped out. ✗ The oscillation is being injected by the command, not left behind by an under-damped loop. D can only react after the fact, and it amplifies encoder noise and heats the motor.

B. Low-pass filter the encoder feedback so the controller stops chasing the wobble. ✗ That hides the symptom from the controller while the payload still shakes, and the added phase lag eats stability margin.

C. Halve the acceleration limit for the whole move. ✗ Shrinks the amplitude and costs cycle time, but the acceleration still steps discontinuously at the corners — the unbounded jerk, which is the actual cause, is untouched.

D. Swap the trapezoid for an S-curve (jerk-limited) profile. ✓ Right — a trapezoid’s acceleration jumps instantaneously at each corner, i.e. infinite jerk, which is an impulse that excites the arm’s structural and drivetrain modes. Ramping acceleration over a finite jerk-limited time bounds that excitation and greatly reduces the residual vibration.

Q: Scout’s elbow, under PID position control, stops about 0.3° short of its setpoint, sits completely still for roughly a second, then abruptly lurches past it, creeps back, and repeats — a slow cycle that never dies out. Turning the integral gain to zero stops the cycling and leaves a small constant offset instead. What is happening?

A. Stiction and integral action are fighting each other: the integrator ramps torque while the joint is stuck, and once it passes breakaway the friction drops to its lower sliding value and the joint shoots past. ✓ Right — this is the classic friction-induced hunting limit cycle, caused by static friction exceeding Coulomb friction plus an integrator that keeps winding up while nothing moves. Fix it with friction feedforward, integrator leakage, a small integral deadband, or dither — not with more gain.

B. The gear train has backlash, so the output sits in the dead zone until the teeth re-engage; fit anti-backlash gears. ✗ Backlash shows up as bounded lost motion on direction reversals, not as a second of complete stillness while torque silently builds before a breakaway.

C. The integral gain is too low to clear the last 0.3°; raise it until the joint reaches the setpoint. ✗ The usual reflex, and it makes things worse — a bigger integrator only reaches breakaway sooner and overshoots harder. No amount of PID tuning removes a stiction-driven limit cycle.

D. The encoder is too coarse, so the controller is chasing quantisation noise around the setpoint. ✗ 0.3° is many counts on any real joint encoder, and quantisation chatter is fast and jittery rather than a slow stick-then-lurch.

29Touching Things — Impedance vs Admittance

In plain words. Position control is the wrong tool the moment Scout's arm touches something. Tell a position controller to move 1 mm into a steel shelf and it will push with everything it has. Instead you control the relationship between pushing and moving — you make the arm behave like a spring.

The rule is simple and symmetric. If the world is hard, the robot must be the soft one, or something breaks. If the world is soft, the robot should be the firm one, or you lose accuracy — a soft, torque-driven arm lets its own friction drag it off target.

On Scout Gripping a cardboard parcel: soft target, so admittance-style control with a force sensor works well — squeeze until you measure the right force. Placing that parcel onto a steel shelf: a hard target, and the honest answer is that Scout’s stiff, non-backdrivable geared arm cannot give you true impedance-style yielding at all. Against steel you go the other way — a heavy, well-damped virtual model (as in the question below), which buys contact stability by making the arm less compliant, not more — and you catch what is left with current-based collision detection. Where the arm can yield, letting it yield on contact rather than pushing through to a commanded position it can never reach.

Why it matters: position control against a rigid body generates unbounded force by construction. So safety around people has to come from changing which quantity you control — not from adding a faster watchdog around a controller that was always going to push. That is why collaborative robots use joint torque sensing and torque control where they can, and on stiff geared arms fall back to current-based collision detection that stops the motion — limiting force, but never yielding to it.

One more line worth memorising: you can never control force and position in the same direction at the same time — you pick one per axis. Polishing a surface wants force control pressing into it and position control sliding along it.

Q: Scout’s arm has a wrist force/torque sensor and stiff, non-backdrivable geared joints, and uses admittance control so staff can hand-guide it. Guiding in free air feels excellent, but the moment the gripper touches a rigid steel shelf the whole arm buzzes and chatters, and it gets worse the lighter they make the virtual mass. What is going on?

A. The force/torque signal is too noisy; add a heavier low-pass filter on it. ✗ It is a self-sustaining oscillation that appears only on contact, so it is loop stability, not sensor noise — and extra filtering adds phase lag inside the loop, which makes contact stability worse.

B. Admittance control loses contact stability against stiff environments, because contact closes a high-gain loop through the environment’s stiffness and the lag of the inner position loop; raise the virtual mass and damping once contact is detected. ✓ Right — admittance control performs well against soft environments and goes unstable against stiff ones; impedance control is the complement — stable against stiff contact but inaccurate in free motion and soft contact, because nothing compensates the joints’ own friction. The practical fix is to trade guiding responsiveness for stability by stiffening the virtual model’s inertia and damping in contact.

C. Raise the virtual stiffness so the arm holds its commanded pose more firmly against the shelf. ✗ Virtual stiffness is not the knob that sets contact stability — the virtual inertia and damping seen through the inner loop’s lag are — and you cannot be simultaneously stiff to the shelf and compliant to the operator through one admittance model.

D. Switch the contact phase over to impedance control. ✗ The right controller for a stiff environment in principle, but impedance control needs backdrivable, torque-controllable joints, and Scout’s geared joints are not — which is why admittance was chosen in the first place.

30SLAM — Building the Map and Finding Yourself at the Same Time

Simultaneous Localisation And Mapping. Everything later in this document assumes Scout has a map. This is where the map comes from.

In one line: SLAM is what a robot does when it has no map and no idea where it is, and has to work out both at once — from nothing but its own wheels and its own sensors.

Every planner from here on starts with the same sentence: “given a map…”. Scout was not handed one. Nobody walked the building with a tape measure. It has two wheel encoders, an inertial sensor, and a LiDAR, and it has to produce the floorplan itself — while driving around inside it.

The chicken and the egg

Here is why this is hard, and it is worth saying slowly because it is the whole problem:

  • To draw a wall on a map, Scout needs to know where it was standing when it saw that wall. A LiDAR does not measure “there is a wall at position (4.2, 7.9)”. It measures “something solid, 2.3 metres that way, from wherever I am”. Turn that into a map and you must already know the pose.
  • To know where it is standing, Scout needs a map to compare against. “I must be here, because this is the only spot where a wall would be 2.3 metres to my left and 5 metres ahead.” That needs a map.

Each one needs the other first. SLAM is the family of methods that refuses to pick and estimates both together.

Why Scout cannot just use its wheels Wheel odometry — counting encoder ticks — is an excellent short-term guess and a hopeless long-term one. The errors never average away: systematic biases accumulate steadily, and even the random part accumulates, because position is the running sum of every step ever taken. A tyre worn a fraction smaller on one side means every single metre driven adds a little turn that never happened. Slip on a smooth floor means ticks with no motion behind them. None of it is recoverable later, because the error compounds: a heading that is wrong by two degrees puts you wrong by half a metre after fifteen, and you carry that error into everything you measure from then on.

Q: Scout has already mapped the warehouse and the map is good. It now needs to know where it is, every day, for the next two years. What should it run?

A. Full SLAM continuously, so the map stays fresh ✗ You would pay the cost of estimating a map you already have, and worse, let a good map be corrupted by a bad day — a trolley parked in an aisle gets written in as a wall.

B. Dead reckoning, since the map is known ✗ Knowing the map does nothing for wheel slip. Odometry drift is unbounded and never recovers on its own; that is the whole reason a map is useful.

C. Localisation only, against the fixed map — AMCL or similar ✓ The map is known, so the only unknown is the pose. That is a far smaller, cheaper and more robust problem. Map once with SLAM, localise every day after.

D. Re-map from scratch at the start of each shift ✗ Expensive, and it throws away a good map in favour of one built in a single session. Re-map when the building genuinely changes, not on a timer.

31Configuration Space — Shrink the Robot to a Dot

In plain words. Planning for a robot with a real width is horrible. So we cheat: grow every obstacle by the robot's own size, and then treat the robot as a single dot. A dot is either inside a forbidden region or it is not — and that is a one-line check.

Why it matters: collision checking is where planners spend roughly 90% of their time. Inflating obstacles once, up front, means every one of the million checks that follows becomes nearly free.

Two consequences worth knowing. For Scout's wheels, inflating is easy — just pad the map by Scout's radius. For Scout's arm, it is not: a tidy box in the room becomes a twisted blob in joint-angle space with no simple formula, which is why arm planners never build the C-space obstacles explicitly and instead poke at C-space randomly (Part VIII).

And the reason that difference exists is the curse of dimensionality. A grid with 10 steps per axis costs 10 cells for one axis, 1,000 for three, and 100,000 for five — and 10 steps is a useless 36° per joint; at 1° a five-joint arm needs 3605, about six trillion. Scout's base has three (x, y, heading) so a grid is fine. Scout's five-joint arm does not. That single fact decides which algorithm you use.

32Costmaps — Turning Walls into Numbers

In plain words. A costmap is a grid where each cell holds not just "blocked or free" but "how much would I rather avoid this?" Cells near a wall are legal but expensive, so Scout naturally drives down the middle of a corridor — like a person would.
On Scout Scout keeps two costmaps. A global one covering the whole office, built from the stored map, updated about once a second. And a local one — a small window a few metres wide that follows Scout around, rebuilt from live LiDAR and depth-camera data 10 to 30 times a second. The person who steps out of a doorway appears first, and at full rate, in the local one.

Why it matters: padding with a fading cost rather than a hard line is what makes paths look sensible. With a hard line, hugging the wall and driving down the middle are equally free, so the planner picks either. With a fading cost, clearance becomes something it trades against distance.

Q: Scout keeps clipping doorframes. The path it is given is genuinely the shortest one on the grid, and the planner has no bugs. What is the right fix?

A. Increase the heuristic weight ✗ That trades optimality for search speed. It changes how hard the planner looks, not what it considers a good path.

B. Slow the robot down near walls ✗ Treats the symptom. A slow robot on a wall-hugging path still needs only one localisation error to make contact — it just makes contact more gently.

C. Switch from A* to RRT ✗ A different planner on the same costmap optimises the same thing. Sampling planners hug obstacles just as happily when nothing penalises it.

D. Add an inflation layer to the costmap so cells near obstacles cost more ✓ The planner is doing exactly what it was asked. Change what it is asked: make proximity to walls expensive and the same search will pay a few extra cells to run down the middle.

34The Grid We Will Use for Everything

Everything in this Part is demonstrated on one small map. Scout starts at S on the left of a corridor and must reach G on the right. A wall of shelving blocks the middle, with gaps above and below. Each cell is one step, and Scout can move up, down, left or right.

Every free cell's guess h - Manhattan blocks to G, pretending the walls are not there.

Before any search runs, we need the guess — usually written h. For each cell it estimates "how many more steps to the goal?". The simplest sensible guess is Manhattan distance: blocks across plus blocks down, ignoring the walls entirely.

h = |my_column - goal_column| + |my_row - goal_row|

Ignoring the walls feels like cheating, but it is exactly right. The one rule a guess must obey is that it must never overestimate the true remaining cost. Pretending walls do not exist can only ever make the guess smaller than the truth, which is the safe direction.

Why "never overestimate" is the magic rule. If a guess is too small, the search may waste a little time exploring somewhere hopeful that turns out badly — harmless. If a guess is too big, the search may dismiss the route that was actually best, and never come back to it. Underestimating costs time; overestimating costs correctness.

35Dijkstra — Flooding Outwards With No Guess

In plain words. Spread out from the start like water filling a floor, always filling the cheapest unexplored cell next. When the water reaches the goal, the route it took is the cheapest one.

Dijkstra keeps one number per cell, called g: the real number of steps taken to get here from S. It repeatedly picks the unexplored cell with the smallest g, then looks at its neighbours and updates their g if it has found a cheaper way in.

g[start] = 0, everything else = infinity
put the start in a queue ordered by g

while the queue is not empty:
    current = pop the cell with the SMALLEST g
    if current is the goal:  done

    for each neighbour up/down/left/right:
        if it is a wall: skip
        new_g = g[current] + 1                  /* one step costs 1 */
        if new_g < g[neighbour]:                /* found a cheaper route in */
            g[neighbour]    = new_g
            came_from[neighbour] = current      /* remember how we got here */
            put neighbour in the queue
Dijkstra. Blue = cells it opened, numbers = real steps from S, pink = the path it found.

Look at what happened. The numbers are the true step count from S. Notice that Dijkstra explored almost the entire grid — including the whole top corridor and the cells above the goal, which cannot help it. It expanded 39 cells to find an 11-step path.

It did that because it has no idea where the goal is. It only knows how far it has come, never how far it has left to go. So it treats "one step north, up the wall" and "one step east, straight at the goal" as equally promising.

Why it matters: that apparent waste is sometimes exactly what you want. Flooding outward from the goal gives every cell in the building its true distance-to-goal — a lookup table Scout can follow downhill from anywhere, which is how the navigation function in §45 is built.

36A* — Dijkstra Plus a Guess

In plain words. Same as Dijkstra, but when deciding which cell to explore next, add the guess of what is left. So you order by "steps already taken plus estimated steps remaining" instead of just "steps already taken".
f = g + h
    ↑   ↑
    |   +-- h: the GUESS of remaining steps (Manhattan, from section 34)
    +------ g: the REAL steps spent getting here

That is the entire difference. The code is Dijkstra's with one term added:

g[start] = 0
push start into the queue with f = h(start)

while the queue is not empty:
    current = pop the cell with the SMALLEST f        /* <-- f, not g */
    if current is the goal:  reconstruct and return the path

    for each neighbour up/down/left/right:
        if it is a wall: skip
        new_g = g[current] + 1

        if new_g < g[neighbour]:
            g[neighbour]         = new_g
            came_from[neighbour] = current
            f = new_g + h(neighbour)                  /* <-- THE ONE LINE */
            push neighbour with that f

return FAILURE      /* the queue emptied: no path exists */
The one line that makes it A*. f = new_g + h(neighbour). Delete the h and you have written Dijkstra. Delete the g and you have written greedy best-first search, which charges at the goal and frequently takes a terrible route. A* is exactly the balance of the two.

Why it matters: here is the proof that A* still finds the best path, and being able to sketch it marks you out. Suppose A* is about to finish with a path of 11 steps, but a 9-step path exists. Some cell on that better path must still be in the queue, and for it, g + h is at most 9, because h never overestimates. So its f is below 11, so A* would have popped it first. Contradiction — the 11 must have been optimal.

The follow-up question. “Never overestimates” is called an admissible heuristic, and it is what most people say. The sharper answer is that with a closed list — the usual implementation, where you never re-open a cell once expanded — admissibility alone is not quite enough; you want the heuristic to be consistent (also called monotone): h(a) ≤ cost(a,b) + h(b) for every neighbouring pair. Consistency implies admissibility, and every heuristic in this document (Manhattan, octile, Euclidean) is consistent, so in practice you rarely hit the difference. Knowing that it exists is the thing being tested.

Q: Scout's grid allows diagonal moves, and someone uses Manhattan distance as the guess. What happens?

A. Nothing — Manhattan is always safe ✗ It is safe only when diagonals are forbidden. Going one diagonal step covers one column and one row, so Manhattan claims 2 where the true cost is about 1.4 — an overestimate.

B. The search may return a path that is not the shortest, without warning ✓ An overestimating guess can make A* dismiss the genuinely best route. It still finds a path, so nothing looks broken — which is what makes this bug nasty. Use octile.

C. A* becomes slower ✗ It usually becomes faster — that is the seduction. It buys speed with correctness.

D. It fails to find any path ✗ Completeness survives. Only optimality is lost, which is exactly the distinction §33 exists to make.

38D* Lite — Repairing Instead of Restarting

In plain words. A door closes, or a trolley appears. Re-running the whole search throws away work that was almost entirely still valid. D* patches only the part that actually changed.

The clever bit is the direction. D* Lite searches backwards, from the goal outwards. Here is why that matters: if you search forwards from Scout, then the instant Scout drives one cell everything you computed is measured from the wrong place and must be redone. But the goal does not move. Root the search there, and all your stored costs stay valid however far Scout travels — only the cells whose stored distance the change actually invalidated need fixing (the ones whose best route ran through it, which are not necessarily the ones nearest the obstacle).

On Scout Honestly, for an office-sized map, most modern stacks just re-run A* ten times a second, because 26-cell searches take microseconds. D* Lite earns its keep on large maps with small, rare changes — a warehouse the size of a football pitch, or a planetary rover. Knowing when not to use it is the better answer.

Why it matters: "search from the goal, because the goal is the thing that is not moving" is the whole insight, and it is what interviewers are listening for.

39Theta* — Escaping the Staircase

In plain words. Grid search can only travel along grid lines, so its paths come out as zigzag staircases — longer than necessary and horrible to drive. Theta* fixes this with one extra check.

When A* is about to record "cell X's parent is cell Y", Theta* first asks: can X see Y's parent directly, in a straight line with nothing in the way? If yes, it skips Y entirely and links X straight to the grandparent. Repeat that everywhere and the staircase collapses into straight runs at any angle.

/* the ONE change from A*, inside the neighbour loop */
if line_of_sight(came_from[current], neighbour):
        /* we can cut the corner -- link to the GRANDparent */
        parent = came_from[current]
else:
        parent = current            /* ordinary A* behaviour */
new_g = g[parent] + straight_line_distance(parent, neighbour)   /* Euclidean, from the chosen parent -- not +1 */

Why it matters: you could instead smooth the staircase afterwards, and many systems do. But smoothing can only tidy the route the grid already committed to — Theta* makes the decision during the search, so it can choose a genuinely different route. The cost is a line-of-sight check per neighbour generated (up to eight per expansion), which is more expensive than a neighbour lookup.

40Hybrid A* — When the Robot Cannot Turn on the Spot

In plain words. Scout can spin in place, so a grid path is fine for it. A car cannot. For car-like robots the path must be drivable, not merely free of obstacles — and that needs a different search.

Hybrid A* changes what a "cell" means. Instead of jumping from cell centre to cell centre, it keeps the robot's real position and heading, and generates each next state by actually simulating the vehicle with a few steering angles — hard left, straight, hard right — for a short distance.

for each steering angle in [-max, 0, +max]:
    simulate the real vehicle forward, and (for a car that can reverse) backward, for a short distance
    new_state = (x, y, heading)          /* continuous numbers, not cell centres */

    cell = which grid cell and heading-bin does new_state fall in?
    if we already have a cheaper state in that same cell+bin:
        discard this one                 /* the grid is used ONLY for pruning */
    else:
        keep it and push onto the queue

So the grid is demoted from "the thing you search" to "a way of throwing away duplicates". You get a grid's efficiency with genuinely drivable motions.

Two extra details make it practical:

  • Two guesses, take the larger. One ignores obstacles but respects the turning circle. The other ignores the turning circle but respects obstacles — and that one is just a Dijkstra flood over the grid, exactly §35. Each alone is badly misleading; the larger of the two is both safe and far better informed.
  • The analytic shortcut. Every so often, just try connecting straight to the goal with a known curve (Reeds–Shepp). If it is clear, you are finished. This turns the slow final wiggling-into-place into one formula, and is the difference between a parking manoeuvre taking 5 seconds and 50 milliseconds.
Curve typeWhat it is
DubinsShortest path for a forward-only vehicle with a minimum turning circle — always arcs and straight lines. Used for fixed-wing drones
Reeds–SheppThe same, but reversing is allowed — which is why cars can parallel-park, and why it is the one used inside Hybrid A*

Why it matters: a path that is collision-free but undrivable cannot be rescued by any controller or any amount of padding. If the planner's idea of the robot is wrong, everything downstream is wrong — which is the single most useful thing to say about this topic.

Q: A car-like warehouse robot keeps getting grid paths it cannot follow, and cuts corners into shelving. Best fix?

A. Plan with heading included, using Hybrid A* or a state lattice, so every path is drivable by construction ✓ Fixes the actual mismatch: the planner's model of the robot was wrong.

B. Increase the obstacle padding ✗ It buys clearance and would reduce crashes, which is tempting — but the path is still impossible to drive, so the controller still cannot follow it.

C. Tune the path follower harder ✗ Asking a controller to follow an impossible path harder makes it saturate and cut corners more, not less.

D. Smooth the path with a spline ✗ A real improvement and a common band-aid — but "smooth" is not the same as "within the turning circle", and nothing guarantees the result respects it.

PART VIII · RANDOM SAMPLING — PLANNING FOR SCOUT'S ARM
What you use when a grid is impossible, which is every robot arm ever built.

41Why Grids Stop Working

Everything in Part VII assumed you could lay a grid over the world. For Scout's base that works: three quantities (x, y, heading), and a grid of a few hundred thousand cells covers a whole office.

Scout's arm has five joints. To plan for it you must search the space of joint angles — five numbers, not three. Ten steps per joint means 105 = 100,000 cells, which sounds fine until you want any real resolution: 50 steps per joint is 312 million cells, and a 7-joint arm at 50 steps is 780 billion. And you would have to run a full collision check on every one.

The key realisation. You do not actually need to know the whole space. You only need to find one safe route through it. So instead of examining every possibility, just throw darts — pick random arm poses, keep the ones that do not collide, and try to join them up.

42PRM — Build a Road Network Once, Reuse It Forever

In plain words. A PRM (Probabilistic RoadMap) is a road network built by throwing darts. Scatter random poses, discard any that collide, then join up nearby survivors. You now have a map of safe roads, and any future journey is just an A* search over that map — exactly the code from §36.
PHASE 1 -- BUILD IT ONCE (offline, takes seconds or minutes):

    repeat N times:                        /* N is typically 1000 - 10000 */
        q = a random arm pose
        if q does not collide:  add it as a node

    for each node:
        for each of its k nearest neighbours:       /* k is typically 5 - 15 */
            if moving in a straight line between them never collides:
                add an edge

PHASE 2 -- ANSWER A JOURNEY (online, milliseconds):

    connect the current pose and the target pose to their nearest nodes
    run A* over the network
On Scout Scout picks parcels from the same store room all day. Build the roadmap once when it is commissioned, and every subsequent "reach into shelf B" is an A* search taking about a millisecond. The expensive collision checking was paid for once, weeks ago.

The one weakness you will be asked about: narrow gaps

Your chance of a random dart landing inside a region is proportional to how big that region is. A wide-open space gets hundreds of samples. A narrow gap — the slot between two shelves that Scout's gripper must pass through — might get none at all. So the roadmap ends up disconnected in exactly the place connectivity matters most.

FixHow it aims the darts better
Gaussian samplingThrow darts in pairs a short distance apart. Keep a point only if one of the pair collides and the other does not — which means you are near a surface, where the interesting gaps are
Bridge testThrow two darts a short distance apart. If both collide but the midpoint between them is free, keep the midpoint. That pattern — blocked, free, blocked — is the unmistakable signature of a narrow gap
Obstacle-basedDeliberately sample near obstacle surfaces rather than uniformly

Why it matters: a PRM stores promises — every edge is a recorded claim that a motion was safe. Move one shelf and an unknown number of those promises become lies, so you must recheck them, which costs exactly what you were trying to save. PRM is for worlds that stay still. RRT is for one journey in a world that does not.

43RRT — Growing a Tree Toward Random Points

In plain words. An RRT (Rapidly-exploring Random Tree) grows like a climbing plant. Pick a random point. Find whichever part of your existing plant is nearest to it. Grow a short shoot from there toward the point. Repeat a few thousand times and the plant fills the space — including, eventually, the goal.
tree = { start }

repeat until you reach the goal (or run out of time):

    /* STEP 1 -- pick a target to grow toward */
    if random() < 0.05:                  /* 5% of the time, aim at the goal */
        q_rand = goal
    else:
        q_rand = a completely random pose

    /* STEP 2 -- find the nearest existing point on the tree */
    q_near = the node in the tree CLOSEST to q_rand         /* <-- the magic */

    /* STEP 3 -- take one small step in that direction */
    q_new = q_near + eps * direction(q_near -> q_rand)

    /* STEP 4 -- keep it only if the motion is safe */
    if the motion from q_near to q_new never collides:
        add q_new to the tree, recording q_near as its parent

    if q_new was added and is within eps of the goal:
        return the chain of parents back to the start

Why step 2 is the whole algorithm

This looks like an innocuous line, but it is what makes RRT work. Because you always grow from the nearest node, a node gets chosen in proportion to how much empty space surrounds it. Nodes buried in the middle of the tree are rarely nearest to anything. Nodes on the outer frontier are nearest to huge regions of unexplored space.

So without ever being told to explore, the tree automatically reaches outward into territory it has not visited. This property has a name — Voronoi bias — and naming it is exactly what interviewers listen for.

Grow two trees, one from the start and one from the goal, and after each random extension of one tree have the other reach greedily toward that newest node until they touch. Two improvements at once: growing from both ends roughly halves how far each must travel, and the "connect" step repeats the extension until something blocks it, rather than creeping one ε at a time. In practice it is often several times faster — Kuffner & LaValle measured a three- to fourfold gain from the connect step, mostly in uncluttered spaces — and it is the default planner for robot arms everywhere.

Honest caveat worth volunteering: plain RRT is not optimal and not even close. Its paths are jagged and often much longer than necessary (how much depends on the problem), because each node's parent is simply whatever happened to be nearest at that moment. In practice you always either shortcut the result afterwards or use RRT*.

44RRT* — the Version That Tidies Up After Itself

In plain words. Plain RRT attaches each new node to the nearest one and never reconsiders, so early bad decisions are permanent. RRT* adds two steps that let later, better information fix earlier mistakes.
after adding q_new to the tree, look at all neighbours within radius r (RRT* lets this radius shrink as the tree grows, like γ(log n / n)^(1/d), so the number of neighbours it has to examine stays around log n; the “approaches the best” guarantee only requires that it shrink no faster than this — a fixed radius keeps the guarantee but costs more per iteration as the tree fills in) — conceptually, all neighbours within radius r:

    /* STEP 1 -- CHOOSE THE BEST PARENT, not merely the nearest */
    for each neighbour within r:
        if cost(neighbour) + dist(neighbour, q_new) < cost(q_new) and the motion neighbour -> q_new never collides:
            re-parent q_new to that neighbour        /* a cheaper way in */

    /* STEP 2 -- REWIRE: could my neighbours do better by going through me? */
    for each neighbour within r:
        if cost(q_new) + dist(q_new, neighbour) < cost(neighbour) and the motion q_new -> neighbour never collides:
            re-parent that neighbour to q_new        /* re-route them */

Step 2 is the interesting one. It means the tree is continuously reorganising itself: a node added at iteration 50 can have its route improved by a node added at iteration 5,000. Given enough time, the whole tree converges on the best possible set of connections.

Informed RRT* adds one more excellent idea. Once you already hold a path of some length, any point that could possibly improve on it must lie inside an ellipse with the start and goal as its two focal points. Sampling anywhere outside that ellipse is provably wasted effort — so do not. And each improvement shrinks the ellipse further, so the search accelerates as it goes.

Q: Scout's arm must reach into a tight shelf. RRT-Connect succeeds 3 times in 10 and otherwise times out. Best first change?

A. Switch to RRT* for a better path ✗ Solves the wrong problem entirely: the complaint is finding any path, and RRT* is slower to its first answer, not faster.

B. Aim at the goal 50% of the time ✗ Turns the planner greedy, so it repeatedly jams against the shelf edge instead of exploring around it — exactly the failure §43 warned about.

C. Increase the step size so the tree grows faster ✗ Feels like speed, but bigger steps are less likely to fit through a tight gap without clipping it — it makes the real problem worse.

D. This is the narrow-gap problem — bias the sampling into the gap and check the collision padding is not over-inflated ✓ Names the known failure and attacks what actually governs the success rate. Over-cautious padding is very often the real culprit — the gap may have been closed in software, not in reality. Note that the bridge test of §42 is a roadmap sampler: for a single-query tree you use the same idea as a sampling bias, and you also shorten the step and bias toward the goal region, because an RRT extends from its nearest node, so where samples land is only half of it.

45Potential Fields — Simple, Tempting, and Broken

In plain words. Pretend the goal is a magnet pulling Scout in, and every obstacle pushes it away. Add up the pulls and pushes and drive whichever way the total points. No searching at all, and about ten lines of code.
every cycle:
    pull = (goal_position - my_position)              /* scaled */
    push = 0
    for each obstacle:
        push += (away from it, stronger the closer you are)
    drive in the direction of (pull + push)

It is beautifully cheap and completely reactive. It is also fundamentally broken as a planner, for one reason: the pushes and pulls can cancel out somewhere that is not the goal. Scout then sits there, perfectly balanced, forever — and because the method has no memory it cannot notice it has been there before, and has no concept of backing out to try another way.

The proper fix: a navigation function

The problem is that a hand-written field can have many low points. So build a field that provably has only one — at the goal. And you already know how: run Dijkstra outward from the goal (§35) across the whole costmap. Every cell then holds its true distance-to-goal.

/* build once per re-plan */
run Dijkstra from the GOAL across the costmap
    -> every cell now holds its true cost-to-go

/* then driving is trivial, and cannot get stuck */
every cycle:
    look at my neighbours
    step to whichever has the LOWEST cost-to-go

There are no false low points, because every cell's value is a genuine distance and distances only decrease toward the goal. You have swapped a clever formula for a computed table, and bought a guarantee.

Why it matters: potential fields are still used everywhere — but as a local layer underneath a real planner, where their cheapness and instant reactivity shine. The global planner decides which side of the shelf to go round; the field handles the last metre. The failure only bites when you ask it to be the whole planner.

Q: Scout uses pure potential fields and oscillates side to side down a narrow corridor, never reaching the end. Why?

A. The goal attraction is too weak ✗ Raising it moves the balance point but not the structure — Scout is still shoved alternately by each wall.

B. The costmap resolution is too coarse ✗ Coarse maps do make the pushes jerky, which is a real issue — but the oscillation persists at any resolution because it is structural.

C. Each wall pushes in turn, and with no damping or memory Scout bounces between them — a known limit cycle of the method ✓ Names the mechanism. Standard fixes: add damping, widen the influence distance, or replace it with a navigation function.

D. Scout is non-holonomic ✗ A genuine source of trouble in general, which makes it plausible — but a robot that can slide sideways shows exactly the same corridor bouncing.

46Smoothing — Polishing a Rough Path

In plain words. Sampling planners produce jagged paths. Rather than searching again, take the path you have and nudge it: push each point away from obstacles, and pull it straighter. Like tightening a rubber band that is snagged on pegs.
repeat many times:
    for each point along the path:
        push   = direction away from the nearest obstacle    /* needs a distance map */
        pull   = direction that makes this point line up with its neighbours
        move the point a little by (push + pull)

The catch is that the push needs a direction, not just a yes/no answer. A plain blocked-or-free grid gives you an infinitely steep cliff and no clue which way to step. This is exactly what an ESDF (Euclidean Signed Distance Field) is for — a map where every cell stores its distance to the nearest obstacle, so the direction to push is simply "uphill".

The second catch is that these methods are local. They will happily tidy a path but cannot decide to go round the other side of a shelf — that would mean jumping across an obstacle, which no amount of nudging will do.

The standard industrial pipeline, worth quoting: sample to find something that works, then smooth to make it good. RRT-Connect finds the route in 50 milliseconds; CHOMP or a simple shortcutter spends the next 50 making it smooth and clear. Neither could do the other's job.

Why it matters: the cheapest smoother of all is shortcutting: repeatedly pick two random points on the path and, if the straight line between them is clear, delete everything in between. Five lines of code, and it removes most of RRT's jaggedness. Mention it — it is what people actually ship.

Curves — the other half of smoothing

Everything above nudges the points you already have. The other approach is to stop treating the path as points at all and replace stretches of it with a curve you can write down. That matters for a reason which is easy to miss: what actually hurts a robot is not a corner in the path, it is a jump in the path’s curvature.

Curvature (κ, kappa) is just 1 / turning radius. A straight line has κ = 0. A tight circle has a big κ. For a car-like robot, curvature is set by the steering angle (κ = tan δ / wheelbase) — so a jump in curvature is a demand to whip the steering to a new angle instantaneously, which no steering rack can do.

That gives three grades of “smooth”, and the words come up in interviews:

GradeWhat is continuousWhat the robot feels
G0Position only — the pieces meetA corner. To follow it exactly the robot must stop and turn on the spot. This is what a raw grid path gives you.
G1Position and headingNo corner, and the robot never stops — but curvature still steps, so the steering is yanked to a new angle at each join. Rounding corners with a single circular arc or a quadratic Bézier gets you here.
G2Position, heading and curvatureThe steering wheel turns at a finite rate. This is what you want for anything that drives fast or carries a person.

Bézier curves — the workhorse

A Bézier curve is defined by a handful of control points, and it is built by nothing more exotic than repeated straight-line blending. To find the point at fraction t: slide along each leg of the control polygon by t, join the points you land on, slide along those legs by the same t, and repeat until one point is left. That is de Casteljau’s algorithm, and it is the whole thing:

def de_casteljau(points, t):
    while len(points) > 1:
        points = [lerp(points[i], points[i+1], t)
                  for i in range(len(points) - 1)]
    return points[0]

def lerp(a, b, t):
    return (a[0] + (b[0] - a[0]) * t,
            a[1] + (b[1] - a[1]) * t)

Three properties are why planners like them:

  • The curve starts at the first control point and ends at the last, so you can pin it to the waypoints you must hit.
  • It leaves along the first leg and arrives along the last, so you control the heading at both ends — that is how you glue one segment to the next without a corner.
  • It never leaves the convex hull of its control points. If the hull is collision-free, the curve is too — which lets a planner collision-check the hull instead of sampling the curve.

Why it matters: a clothoid is the curve whose curvature changes at a constant rate — which is, for small steering angles, what happens when you drive at a steady speed and turn the steering wheel at a constant rate. That is not a coincidence, and it is the reason motorway exits are built from them. If an interviewer asks why you would pay for clothoids over Béziers, that is the answer: the clothoid matches how the actuator actually moves.
A practical note that catches people out: smoothing can push the path into an obstacle. The curve cuts inside the corner it is rounding, so anything you smooth must be collision-checked again afterwards — and if it fails, you either shrink the fillet or re-plan. Smoothing is not a free post-processing step.
PART IX · LOCAL PLANNING & FOLLOWING THE PATH
The layer that turns a route into wheel speeds — and reacts to the person who just stepped out.

47Global and Local — Two Planners, Two Jobs

In plain words. One planner looks at the whole building and decides "go left round the kitchen". Another looks only a few metres ahead and decides "step round this person". They run at completely different rates and answer completely different questions.

The reason for splitting is the same as cascade control in §12: each layer gets information and a deadline it can actually meet. Re-planning the whole office 20 times a second is impossible. Reacting to a pedestrian once a second is dangerous. So you do both, at their natural rates.

On Scout — the important contract The local planner is allowed to disagree with the global route. The global path was computed from a map that is already out of date. If the local layer were forced to follow it exactly, one unexpected trolley would deadlock Scout forever. The correct arrangement: the global path is a suggestion about which way round, and the local planner may depart from it so long as it keeps making progress along it.

Why it matters: both layers fail regularly, so you need an explicit recovery ladder: clear the costmap, spin on the spot to take a fresh look, back up, then give up and ask for help. A navigation stack with no answer for "stuck" eventually just stands still forever — the most common field failure there is.

Q: Halfway down a corridor, a person steps into Scout’s path. Which planner should react, and on what timescale?

A. Re-run the global planner over the whole building ✗ Correct in principle, far too slow in practice, and mostly wasted — the rest of the building has not changed. By the time a building-wide search returns, the person has moved.

B. The local planner, inside its small window, at a much higher rate — the global plan is left alone ✓ This is the split. The global plan is advice computed once from the map; the local planner drives, looks only at its immediate surroundings, and its job is to get past and rejoin.

C. Neither — stop until the person leaves ✗ Safe and useless. A robot that stops for every pedestrian never completes a delivery in an occupied building.

D. The global planner, but only over the corridor ✗ A global planner restricted to a local window, re-run constantly, is a local planner. That is a description of the answer, not an alternative to it.

48DWA — Choosing a Speed, Not a Path

In plain words. DWA (Dynamic Window Approach) asks one question every cycle: of all the speeds and turn rates Scout could physically reach in the next instant, which is best? It tries each, simulates a second or two forward, throws away anything that would crash, and scores what is left.

The clever part is where it searches. Rather than searching over positions, DWA searches over (forward speed, turn rate) pairs — because that is where Scout's limits naturally live. "I cannot accelerate faster than 1 m/s²" is simply a rectangle in that space, so every candidate inside the rectangle is achievable by construction. Nothing infeasible can even be proposed.

every cycle (10-30 Hz):

  /* 1. THE DYNAMIC WINDOW -- everything reachable in one timestep */
  v_min = max(0,        current_v - a_max*dt)
  v_max = min(V_LIMIT,  current_v + a_max*dt)
  w_min = max(-W_LIMIT, current_w - alpha_max*dt)
  w_max = min(+W_LIMIT, current_w + alpha_max*dt)

  best = none
  for v in v_min .. v_max:                  /* typically a 10 x 20 sample grid */
      for w in w_min .. w_max:

          /* 2. SIMULATE this choice forward ~2 seconds */
          trajectory = roll_out(current_pose, v, w, horizon)

          /* 3. THE SAFETY TEST -- could I still stop in time? */
          d = distance along trajectory to the first obstacle
          if v > sqrt(2 * d * a_max):        /* braking distance exceeds clearance */
              discard                        /* this option is inadmissible */

          /* 4. SCORE the survivors */
          score =  a * heading_toward_goal(trajectory)
                +  b * clearance(trajectory)
                +  c * speed(v)
          if score > best: best = (v, w)

  /* 5. execute the winner for ONE cycle only, then repeat */
  send(best)

TEB (Timed Elastic Band) is the other common choice. It treats the path as a stretchy band of timed points and repeatedly tugs it — shorter, faster, further from obstacles, always drivable. Because it optimises the whole trajectory under explicit kinematic constraints (including a minimum turning radius), it handles car-like robots and planned reversing manoeuvres, which a one-step (v, ω) search like DWA cannot.

49Following the Path: Pure Pursuit

In plain words. You have a path; something must turn it into steering. Pure pursuit is exactly how you follow someone walking ahead: pick a point a fixed distance ahead on their route, and steer toward it.

One setting controls everything: L, how far ahead to look. And it has a genuine conflict built in:

LookaheadBehaviour
Too shortTracks the path tightly, but Scout wobbles — it over-corrects toward a point right under its nose
Too longBeautifully smooth, but Scout cuts corners — it chords across bends rather than following them
The standard fixScale L with speed: L = k * speed, with a floor and a ceiling. Fast on straights means smooth; slow into corners means tight

Corner-cutting is geometry, not a badly chosen gain — the error grows roughly with L² divided by the corner radius. You manage it; you do not tune it away.

Stanley is the main alternative: it combines "am I pointing the right way?" with "how far off the line am I?", measured at the front axle. Because it answers to heading error as well as lateral error, it pulls onto the line harder and has no lookahead distance to tune at all (on a constant-curvature bend it still carries a steady offset, unless you add a curvature feedforward term), and it won the DARPA Grand Challenge. It is fussier at very low speed.

Why it matters: path following is where an infeasible plan finally shows itself. If the planner ignored Scout's turning limits, no amount of tuning here will help — the follower will simply saturate and cut the corner. Tracking problems are very often planning problems in disguise.

Q: Scout uses pure pursuit, wobbles about the path on straights, but corners smoothly. Best fix?

A. Increase the lookahead distance ✓ Wobbling on straights is the classic sign of looking too close. Better still, scale it with speed, which fixes this without bringing back corner-cutting.

B. Decrease the lookahead distance ✗ Exactly backwards — and tempting, because looking closer does mean tighter tracking. It will make the wobble worse.

C. Add an integral term on the off-path distance ✗ That fights a steady offset, but the problem is oscillation — and integral action adds lag, which usually makes oscillation worse.

D. Re-plan the global path more often ✗ The path is fine; the follower is not.

What is the integral term, in code?error_sum += error * dt — a running total of every error so far. That is why it has memory, and why it can keep pushing when the present error is nearly zero. It is also why it winds up when the motor is maxed out.
What is the derivative term, in code?(error - last_error) / dt — just this error minus the last one. That is why it sees a trend, and also why one noisy sensor reading throws it wildly off.
Why does D need a filter?Differentiating multiplies a signal by its frequency, so it blows up noise. Filter it, and take the derivative of the measurement rather than the error so a new command does not kick the motor.
Phase margin vs gain marginPhase margin = how much extra delay you could survive, read where the response is life-size. Gain margin = how much bigger everything could get, read where the lag hits half a wobble. Different speeds, different accidents. Aim for 45–60° and 6–12 dB.
Why is delay so dangerous?It does not change the size of anything, so it is invisible on a magnitude plot — and the lag it costs grows the faster you run. 5 ms at a 20 Hz crossover is 36°, most of a typical design's whole margin. And adding gain makes it worse, not better.
Anti-windup in one lineStop adding to the memory while the motor is maxed out and the error would push it further in. Or cap the memory so that on its own it can just hold the heaviest expected load — sizing it from physics rather than guessing is the tell.
Why tune inner loops first?Tuning an outer loop assumes the inner one already behaves. Tune position first and every later change to the speed loop undoes your work. Current, then speed, then position.
Why is feedforward free?It sits outside the loop, so it cannot move a single pole and cannot make you unstable in the linear sense. It just means feedback has far less left to do. Two things still bite: a big feedforward term can saturate the actuator, and you cannot invert a non-minimum-phase plant.
Sample rate rule10 to 30 times faster than how fast the loop responds — not the textbook "twice the frequency", which only stops signals being misread. Control also needs the sampling delay to be negligible.
What makes A* optimal?A guess that never overestimates the remaining cost. Multiply that guess by 3 and you get a path guaranteed within 3× the best — often a great trade, but say the bound.
Dijkstra vs A*A* is Dijkstra plus a guess. Same cost when the guess is admissible — not necessarily the same route, since only optimality of cost is guaranteed — for far less work. Remove the guess and you have Dijkstra; ignore the cost-so-far and you have greedy, which is fast and frequently wrong.
Right guess for a grid?Octile distance on an 8-direction grid. "Blocks across plus blocks down" overestimates when diagonals are allowed, and silently breaks optimality.
RRT vs PRMPRM builds a reusable road network offline — right for a static cell answering many journeys. RRT plans one journey on demand — right when the world changes, because PRM's stored roads become lies the moment something moves.
Why is RRT good at exploring?Picking the nearest node to a random point means nodes get chosen in proportion to the empty space around them — and edge nodes have the most. So the tree pulls itself into unexplored space automatically.
Why only 5% goal bias?More makes it greedy, so it jams against the first obstacle in the goal direction and stops exploring around it.
RRT* in one lineRRT plus "pick the best parent, not the nearest" and "re-route neighbours through me if that is cheaper". Honest caveat: it converges slowly, so RRT-Connect plus shortcutting often wins inside a 100 ms budget.
The narrow-gap problemYour chance of randomly landing in a gap is proportional to its size, so tight gaps almost never get sampled. Fixes: aim the sampling at surfaces and gaps — and check your collision padding is not over-inflated, which is very often the real culprit.
Why not potential fields alone?Pushes and pulls can cancel somewhere that is not the goal, and with no memory it cannot back out. Excellent as a local layer; fatal as the whole planner. The proper fix is a Dijkstra flood from the goal, which has only one low point by construction.
Holonomic vs non-holonomicHolonomic can move any direction instantly. Non-holonomic cannot — a car cannot slide sideways — so a collision-free grid path may be undrivable, and you need Hybrid A*.
Dubins vs Reeds–SheppBoth are shortest paths with a minimum turning circle. Dubins is forward-only (fixed-wing drones); Reeds–Shepp allows reversing, which is why cars can parallel-park.
Why fade the costmap padding?A hard line makes hugging the wall and driving down the middle equally free. A fading cost makes clearance something the planner trades against distance, so paths look sensible and leave room for tracking error.
Global and local disagree — who wins?Local, for immediate safety. The global path is a suggestion about which way round, not an order. If the local layer cannot make progress, escalate to a global re-plan, then to recovery behaviours.
Impedance vs admittanceHard surface → the robot must be soft → impedance (measure position, command force, needs a backdrivable robot). Soft surface → the robot must be firm → admittance (measure force, command position, needs a force sensor). Backwards against steel is a classic instability.
Why does LQR lose its margins?Plain LQR guarantees at least 60° of margin, free. Add an observer to estimate states you cannot measure and that guarantee vanishes entirely.
The curse of dimensionalityA grid of 10 steps per axis costs 10 cells for one axis, a thousand for three, ten million for seven. That one fact is why grids own wheeled robots and random sampling owns arms.

55Subjective Round — Model Answers

1. "The arm oscillates at the end of every move. Walk me through diagnosing it."

First I separate control from mechanics by changing one thing: if the oscillation frequency moves when I change the gains, it is the loop; if it stays at exactly the same frequency, it is the machine ringing. If it is the loop, I look at what recently ate my timing margin — someone adding a filter, a slower loop rate, extra bus latency, or a raised gain — and I would measure the actual loop period rather than trusting the configured number. If it is mechanical, I stop exciting it rather than trying to fight it: ease the acceleration with an S-curve, notch the resonance, and keep the loop's speed well below it. The reason that split matters: a controller running far slower than a resonance physically cannot damp it, so turning up the derivative gain there only adds noise and heat.

2. "How would you choose between PID, LQR and MPC?"

I start from what I actually have rather than what sounds impressive. No trustworthy model, one input and one output: PID plus whatever feedforward I can compute, because it needs nothing and anyone can maintain it. Genuinely coupled states — a drone, a balancing robot — then LQR, because separate PIDs per axis just ignore the coupling and I would spend the project detuning to hide it, and because LQR's weights are trade-offs I can state physically. MPC only when hard limits are the essence of the problem rather than an afterthought, since it optimises subject to those limits instead of clipping afterwards and patching with anti-windup. And I would say the costs out loud: LQR needs a model and loses its margin guarantee the moment I add an observer; MPC needs a solver that finishes every single cycle.

3. "Design the navigation stack for an indoor delivery robot."

Three layers on three timescales: a global planner over a stored costmap about once a second to decide which way round the building; a local planner at ten to twenty hertz on a small live map, free to deviate from the global route as long as it keeps making progress; and wheel speed loops at a few hundred hertz underneath. Localisation runs against a map built by SLAM, with the transform tree as the spine, because nearly every navigation bug I have seen turns out to be a transform-timing bug. I would pad the costmap with a fading cost rather than a hard line, so clearance gets traded against distance and the robot drives down the middle of corridors. And I would design the recovery ladder deliberately — clear the map, spin to take a fresh look, back up, then give up and ask for help — because a stack with no answer for "stuck" eventually just stands still forever, which is the most common field failure of all.

4. "The arm's planner works in simulation and fails on the real robot. Where do you look?"

Almost always the model of the world rather than the algorithm. So: is the grasped object attached so the planner knows it is there; is the collision padding so cautious that the real gap is now unreachable; does the collision geometry match the actual robot including cables and covers. Then I separate planning failure from execution failure — a path that plans fine but was never timed against the real joint limits will be tracked badly or rejected outright. Then the narrow-gap question: if the target is inside a tight shelf, the success rate is governed by where the random samples land, so better-aimed sampling helps where a longer timeout does not. The reason for that order: sampling planners fail silently and differently every run, so I want to know whether it cannot find a path, or found a wrong one, before touching any parameter.

5. "How do you make a robot safe when it touches a person?"

I put the safety in the structure rather than in a check that might be late. Torque limits live in the innermost current loop, where no amount of position error can ever exceed them. The arm runs the most compliant control its hardware allows — on stiff geared joints like Scout's that is admittance control, not true impedance — so it behaves as springy as it can and gives way on contact instead of pushing through to a commanded position. True impedance control needs genuinely backdrivable, torque-controlled joints, because a heavily geared arm cannot behave softly on torque commands alone (admittance with a force sensor is the slower workaround, and the one that goes unstable against a hard surface) — friction swamps the command. On top I would add a disturbance observer to spot unexplained load, which detects a collision without any force sensor, plus slowing down as a person approaches. The reason it has to be structural: position control against a rigid body generates unbounded force by construction, so the fix must change which quantity you control — not add a faster watchdog around a controller that was always going to push.

6. "Tell me about a control or planning bug you found." — the shape, whatever your story

Five beats, one sentence each. The symptom and what it cost ("it rang at 40 Hz, but only with the payload fitted"). The instrumentation you added to see it (logged the real loop period, commanded versus measured current, and ran a frequency sweep instead of only a step test). The chain of hypotheses — including one that was wrong, and how the evidence killed it. The root cause named precisely ("a 4-sample averaging filter added 1.5 samples of delay, costing 30 degrees of margin"). And the prevention that outlived the fix (an alarm on loop-period jitter, and margin checked automatically rather than only overshoot). This question is never really about the bug — it is an audit of your method, and the wrong-hypothesis beat is what proves the method is real.

How to use this in the room
Answer in three beats: what it is → why it exists → one trade-off or war story. In control, always name what you are trading — speed against noise, accuracy against effort, response against safety margin — because tuning questions are really trade-off questions in disguise. In planning, always name what you are giving up: the guarantee of finding a path, the guarantee it is the best one, or the guarantee the robot can actually drive it. When an option contains "always" or "never", it is almost certainly the wrong one. And when you are stuck, reason out loud from first principles — interviewers grade the why-chain, not the recall.
The five answers that fail interviews
(1) "I would just increase the gain" — gain supplies no timing, and raising it moves you to a faster crossover where even less timing remains. (2) "A faster processor will fix the timing" — real time is about the worst case, not the average. (3) "A* is always optimal" — only with a guess that never overestimates, and the obvious grid guess often does. (4) "RRT finds the shortest path" — it finds a path; plain RRT's path is often noticeably longer than necessary, and how much depends entirely on the problem. (5) "The controller will handle it" — a controller cannot follow an impossible path, so if the planner ignored the turning circle or the acceleration limit, no amount of tuning will rescue it.
